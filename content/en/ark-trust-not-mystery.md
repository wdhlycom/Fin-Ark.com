## 1. Trusts have been mythologized

Mention "family trust" and many instinctively feel it's the privilege of the ultra-wealthy — either look up to it, or think "not my concern." We want to bring it down from the altar: a trust is just a legal tool, in the same family as insurance and wills — it uses a contract to fix "who gets the money, how, and when."

## 2. One decision diagram

Ask yourself three questions:

1. Is your investable asset far beyond the threshold to set up and maintain a trust (usually in the millions-of-USD range)?
2. Do you have complex distribution needs (multiple children, cross-generational, anti-squandering, anti-divorce)?
3. Are you willing to keep paying non-trivial annual structure-maintenance costs?

Only if all three are "yes" does a trust truly make sense for you.

## 3. For most people, the first question is the wall

The reality: most middle-class and new-rich families simply don't have the asset scale to support a real trust. Forcing one on isn't succession — it's burning money yearly to maintain a structure.

And "passing money on by your wishes" doesn't necessarily require a trust. **A well-designed Hong Kong savings policy** — through beneficiary designation, policy splitting, and successor insured — achieves a similar effect, at zero maintenance cost.

## 4. What we think of the "poor man's family trust" label

This phrase is often used for the succession function of HK insurance. It's not just a marketing analogy — it holds structurally: designate beneficiary = directed distribution; policy split = divide one into several for different people; successor insured = cross-generational continuation. The cost is less fine-grained control than a trust, but for most families that's enough.

> No tool is inherently superior — only more or less suited. The real question is "which matches my scale and needs," not "which sounds more advanced."
