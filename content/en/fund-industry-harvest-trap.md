> Someone held public funds, famous private funds, and bank wealth products for years — then pulled out the year-end statement and found something absurd: the fund company sends good-news reports every year, yet their own principal shrank. This divergence isn't accidental; it's the inevitability of the industry's underlying structure.

I often tell friends: the fund business has a contradiction you can't get around.

🔑 **1. The root conflict of interest**

Investors hope for asset appreciation. But a fund company's revenue mainly comes from "scale," not "performance."

As long as assets under management (the jargon is AUM) are large enough, even if the fund's net value is cut in half, the company still collects a fixed management fee of about 1.2%–1.5% a year.

This means: the more it falls, the more reluctant holders are to cut losses, the more scale stays put, and the more securely the fund company collects its money. **For many people, their interests were never the same as the company's, from day one.**

<center>✦ ✦ ✦</center>

🌏 **2. Three underlying logics of the harvest**

## Scale orientation (the AUM model).

A fund company's biggest KPI is "assets under management," not "how much it helped people earn." The bigger the scale, the higher the annual fixed management fee — a revenue curve nearly unaffected by ups and downs. So when the market is worst, it works hardest to "comfort holders into holding long-term" — because it needs that scale to stay.

## Building gods and issuing at the top.

When the market is most frenzied and assets most expensive (like the 2020–2021 peak), that's precisely when fund companies flood the airwaves and issue the most new funds. Retail investors caught the top, becoming contributors to the scale. When the tide recedes, the "star fund manager" and "dazzling historical performance" touted at issuance mostly sank along with the net value.

S&P's SPIVA report has tracked this long-term: over the past 15 years, more than 90% of US actively managed funds underperformed the S&P 500. In other words, most "professional stock-picking," long-term, does worse than buying a basket of indices.

## Style drift and frequent churning.

Chasing hotspots for short-term rankings: betting on new energy today, AI tomorrow. The cost is hefty trading commissions, bid-ask spreads, and impact costs — all paid by the investor, with returns that may not keep up.

<center>✦ ✦ ✦</center>

⚖️ **3. An often-overlooked arithmetic problem**

Suppose you invest one million, the fund's annual management fee is 1.5%, and the real annualized return is 7%.

On the surface, 7% isn't bad. But management fees, subscription/redemption fees, and internal turnover costs combined, the actual long-term annualized you take home may only be 4%–5%.

If the market itself only delivers 2%–3% beta (market average return), after fees you'll most likely underperform "doing nothing, buying a low-cost index."

The fees look small. But over two or three decades of compounding, they quietly eat a sizable chunk of the final value.

<center>✦ ✦ ✦</center>

🏛️ **4. How a wealth-preserver breaks out**

## First, drop the fantasy that "a fund manager can get rich for you."

Funds aren't unbuyable — but put them in the right place: use low-cost, transparent tools (like broad-based indices, bonds) as the base, not stake your life on "the next star."

**Then peel a portion of capital out of "high-fee, high-volatility" equity and shift toward clearer-structured assets.** Assets more worth considering are those with these traits:

- **Extremely low management cost**: no layered commissions;
- **Transparent underlying assets**: you know where the money went;
- **Certain cash flow**: not valued on a story, but with real inflows like interest and dividends;
- **Legal isolation attributes**: when the business fails, family private wealth isn't dragged down with it.

Hong Kong offshore high-rated bonds and multinational large savings-insurance policies are two such tools commonly used in this structure. Their selling point isn't a "high-yield story" but "low volatility, predictable, protected by common law."

<center>✦ ✦ ✦</center>

💡 **5. Four actionable conclusions**

- **Count the fees first, then talk returns**: for any product, ask clearly what "management fee + custody fee + subscription/redemption fee + hidden turnover cost" adds up to, before discussing returns.
- **Beware "top-of-market new issues"**: when the market is jubilant and everyone recommends a fund, stay calm for three days first.
- **Use low-cost tools as the base**: money you won't need long-term, prioritize low-fee indices or bonds, not bets on active funds.
- **Put "defense" before "offense"**: preserving principal structure matters far more than chasing excess returns.

Once the accounts are clear, a lot of "professional" halos dim on their own.
