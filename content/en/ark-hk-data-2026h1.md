## 1. A set of counterintuitive data

In the first half of 2026, new premium from onshore visitors buying insurance in Hong Kong kept climbing, hitting a recent high. The interesting part: the more regulators emphasize "risk," the more the public sphere fills with "offshore scam" talk — yet the more people vote with their feet.

It defies intuition, but it fits logic.

## 2. Not herd behavior — the demand is real

Currency hedging, USD assets, children's education, cross-border retirement — these needs don't vanish because of a single risk warning. They just move from "open discussion" to "private allocation," from "posting receipts on Moments" to "a serious topic at the family dinner table."

We've met too many families who weren't lured by anyone — they did the math, thought it through, and decided.

## 3. The real motivation we see

After deep talks with dozens of families, what they're buying was never "high returns" — anyone chasing those went to trade crypto long ago. What they're buying is **certainty**: money in a policy, written into a contract, denominated in USD, untouched by the swings of any single market's policy.

In an age of rising uncertainty, "certainty" itself is a scarce asset.

## 4. Beware two extreme narratives

The first is the sales pitch that "HK insurance cures everything" — we firmly oppose it; it shames the whole industry. The second is the label that "all offshore assets are scams" — equally harmful, replacing analysis with emotion.

The rational stance is always: see its structure, costs, and applicable boundaries, then decide whether to want it — not be pulled by either extreme.

> The more emotional the debate around a field, the more we need a structural eye to take it apart — not slogans to pick a side.
