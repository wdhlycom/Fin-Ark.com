> Gilts are, in many minds, the very symbol of "safe." Yet in 2022, Britain taught the whole world a lesson.

A friend who works in fixed income once told me: as long as the state doesn't fall, the debt doesn't die.

That holds in most years. But in September 2022, that former "empire on which the sun never set" — the birthplace of global financial rules — gave high-net-worth investors a brutal fall. UK gilts (government bonds) suffered an unprecedented sell-off, at one point pushing the pension system to the edge of collapse.

🔑 **1. Even the safest debt can collapse in an instant**

The trigger was the government's "mini-budget" at the time.

The logical hole was plain: they wanted to spend big while sharply cutting revenue, with the gap filled entirely by issuing debt. The market took one look, saw debt growing far faster than the ability to repay, and voted with its feet.

## A run on credit often isn't about the state going bankrupt — it's about losing confidence in "how interest rates are priced."

Worse was what followed. As bond prices crashed, leveraged institutions — pension funds, for instance — were forced to post more margin. To post margin, they could only sell more gilts. More selling pushed prices lower, which forced more urgent margin calls. A death spiral took shape in an instant.

This friend later asked me: if even the top-tier government debt can't be escaped, then staking your entire fortune on a single sovereign's credit — isn't that betting your whole family's life on one government's "quality of decision-making"?

🌏 **2. A trap disguised as "safety"**

Master Nao, when explaining "The Underlying Logic of Finance," said something: risk never disappears; it only moves.

Some friends on the mainland hold "government-credit" products and long-term debt that, at their cores, carry the shadow of sovereign or quasi-sovereign credit. In sunny weather they stand rock-solid; but when the wind really comes, they hide three fatal flaws:

- Low liquidity: when risk arrives, you simply can't sell.
- The illusion of rigid redemption: someone always thinks "the authorities will step in." Yet before systemic risk, sacrificing part to save the whole is the inevitable move in every game.
- Silent erosion of purchasing power: the face value gets paid, but if credit is damaged and the currency craters (as the pound did then), real wealth is halved all the same.

📌 **3. Safety comes from hedging, not from holding**

Twenty years of experience taught me one plain truth: safety doesn't come from "which one you hold," but from "hedging across jurisdictions."

Some build their career on the mainland while anchoring 100% of their assets to the same sovereign credit. That's "risk resonance" — when the shock comes, there's not even room to maneuver.

That's why I've always pushed Hong Kong. Two hard structural reasons:

- Legal isolation: a policy or trust structured in Hong Kong is protected by the common-law system, with a physical firewall standing between it and any single administrative order.
- Global penetration: a good Hong Kong financial structure often spreads its underlying assets across the world — US Treasuries, quality corporate bonds, even gold. That avoids over-reliance on any single sovereign credit.

💡 **4. Build a "multi-sovereign" mindset**

I read primary foreign-media sources often, and I've found a pattern: in the balance sheets of top families, there is never just one kind of government bond.

Using Hong Kong as a window, they build a "sovereign-credit portfolio" — some US Treasuries to hedge liquidity, some Hong Kong-dollar assets to hedge geographic risk, some physical assets to hedge credit collapse.

## This is what I mean by "institutional arbitrage."

Using Hong Kong as a springboard is, at its core, jumping from a confined, single-risk pool into a global ocean where you can pick your own margin of safety.

In this age without an anchor, so-called "safety" is fragile. As guardians of wealth, we should no longer worship any single endorsement. When even "the safest debt" can't be trusted, only "physical offshore" and "logical hedging" remain the final moat.
