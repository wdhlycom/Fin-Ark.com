## 1. Where the phrase "digital gold" came from

Bitcoin is often called "digital gold." This analogy isn't marketing fluff — it has clear financial logic: **like gold, its supply is finite, it depends on no issuer, and in an era of fiat over-issuance it's treated as a value anchor.** The only difference: gold is physical, Bitcoin is digital.

> When institutions start putting Bitcoin on their allocation sheets, they're not buying a "get-rich-quick dream" — they're buying "scarcity that depends on no one."

## 2. Three logics behind institutions allocating to Bitcoin

**1. Scarcity as an inflation hedge.** Bitcoin's total supply is 21 million, halving every four years, with a predictable issuance curve. In an age of unlimited central-bank easing, this "written into math" scarcity has natural appeal to institutions.

**2. Low-correlation portfolio allocation.** Bitcoin's correlation with stocks and bonds fluctuates across periods, but long-term it provides the scarce "alternative exposure" traditional portfolios lack — a modest allocation can improve the risk-return profile.

**3. Sovereign-credit hedge.** After institutions like BlackRock and Fidelity launched spot Bitcoin ETFs, "compliant coin-buying" became real. Institutions aren't betting on price surges — they're betting that "digital currency will become part of the global reserve system."

## 3. Why it's "institutional-grade," not "retail-grade"

The key point: institutions hold via **ETFs and custodied accounts**, solving the three retail pain points — safe custody, compliant reporting, liquidity. Since 2024, US spot Bitcoin ETF net inflows have repeatedly hit records, marking its shift from "marginal asset" to "allocatable asset."

But note: institutions' "allocation" is usually only a few percentage points of the portfolio. The mainstream institutional attitude toward Bitcoin is — **you can allocate a little, but you can't afford not to study it.**

## 4. What this means for ordinary families

Our stance is clear: yes, Bitcoin is "digital gold," but it's **high-volatility gold.** For ordinary families, its proper role is a small slice of alternative assets (no more than 3% of total assets), not the mainstay.

More important is the repatriation logic: **"quick money" earned from crypto should be taken off the table in time and poured into the most stable Hong Kong insurance pool to lock in returns.** Let the vanguard be the vanguard, and let the home base be the home base.

## 5. In closing

Bitcoin's entry into institutional allocations is a landmark event in financial history. It reminds us: the world is changing, the boundaries of assets are expanding. But change or not, ordinary families' allocation discipline can't break — see the trend clearly, participate in small proportions, and hand certainty over to Hong Kong insurance. That is the clear-headed posture for facing the new world.
